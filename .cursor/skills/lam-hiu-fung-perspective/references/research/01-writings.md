## Model A — the three-point stage clock

Source: LN07 slides 3–7, first-hand slide text. Also drawn onto LN10 slides 60–62.

He defines three points in time, not three ratios:

1. First dollar point: the time of the first order, "your business gaining first dollar revenue."
2. Breakeven point: the time when monthly revenue covers monthly expense.
3. Return-on-investment point: the time when cumulative net profit equals the investment amount. This is payback of capital, not an IRR.

He then maps stages:

- Before first dollar: startup, 0 to 1.
- First dollar to breakeven: scale-up, 1 to 10.
- Through the ROI point: growing, 10 to 100+, which he also labels the mature period on the same diagram.

Only after that growing stage does he start a six-phase business cycle: expansion, peak, recession, depression, trough, recovery (LN07 slides 13–19). His question on the cycle slide is "How to develop your company sustainably?" and "Is fundraising suitable for sustainable development?" He draws "increase resources" on the way back to expansion. He does not answer the question on the slide.

Investor questions he puts to the room (LN07 slide 7), unanswered in the extract:

- Which stage would you invest in, and why?
- Which stage is the lowest cost?
- Which stage is the lowest risk?
- Which stage has the significant increase in cost?

The book (pp.61–65) restates first dollar, breakeven volume, and ROI with HKD arithmetic, then lists marketplace, subscription, freemium, direct sales, advertising, licensing, and on-demand models, plus Osterwalder's canvas and Ash Maurya's lean canvas. Those model types are standard. The stage clock and the four investor questions are the part that is his course.

## Model B — whole product, then MVP

Source: LN06 slides 6–13 and 18–22. Book pp.53–54 expands the same idea and slides toward a generic canvas.

Slide 6 quotes the whole product as "the full set of product and service features required to meet the customer segment's reason for buying," attributed to Jeffrey Moore, "Crossing the Divide" (he means *Crossing the Chasm*). Different segments have different wholes. The whole is not every optional feature. It is the complete set required for that reason to buy. Product/market fit is this match.

He then splits versions (slides 8–9, 13):

- General-purpose / universal: core function only.
- Expected: availability, reliability, performance the buyer assumes.
- Enhanced: support, training, integration, warranty.
- Potential: not built yet.

Worked picture on the slides: an IT product (hardware, software, pre-sales, after-sales, complementary products) and an EV (compliance, range, after-sales, battery life at the charger, autonomy), including a NIO battery-swap version and a Tesla roadster version. Future versions on the slide are speculative (unmanned taxis, "electric vehicles for the elderly").

MVP, slide 18, is Eric Ries: maximum validated learning with minimum effort. He spells the name "Eric Reese" on the slide. Slide 22 is the operating rule: list every candidate feature on the whole-product canvas, including unvalidated ones, then keep only the minimum must-haves. New features are added later as new hypotheses, not as part of the original whole.

LN04 slides 13–15 sit underneath this. A business model is a set of assumptions. Test desirability (the customer's problem), feasibility (technically possible), and viability (financially and commercially viable). He tells researchers and technology-transfer staff to learn lean in order to bridge the lab and the market, and he stacks three canvases: value proposition, customer expansion, business model. The book's lean chapter adds Build-Measure-Learn, a sum-of-squares "error" between features and satisfaction, and JTBD utility weights. Those equations are book-layer. The three-way test and the lab-to-market purpose are on the slides.

## Model C — market arithmetic before the story

Source: LN02 slides 8, 14, 38–39. Book pp.16–22.

He makes students compute, in HKD:

- IoT sensor modules for industrial automation in Hong Kong. Regional TAM assumed at HKD 50 billion. Industrial automation as 30% → SAM HKD 15 billion. Realistic share 5% → SOM HKD 0.75 billion. Porter notes on the same slide: rivalry high (more than 10 competitors), new entrants moderate because of capital, supplier power high for unique components, buyers moderate and they want customization, substitutes are newer wireless sensors. Strategic conclusion on the slide: differentiate, and ally with suppliers. Do not compete on price alone.
- Wearable heart-rate monitor, Hong Kong, "aligned with technology applications in Electrical and Electronic Engineering." Population 7.5 million, adults 18–65 about 5 million, 60% interested → 3 million people, ASP HKD 2,000 → TAM HKD 6 billion. Urban tech-savvy 50% → SAM HKD 3 billion. Capture 2% of SAM in three years → SOM HKD 60 million.
- Slide 38: bottom-up units = customers × units per customer; each SAM and SOM fraction must be backed by research or a stated assumption.

The book adds SWOT, PEST, Porter, Kano, and a customer journey, with Tesla and DJI as the cases, plus a Metcalfe's-law sketch. Kano and the journey are in the LN02 chapter title (slide 48), so they are on the syllabus even though the diagram text did not extract. The sensor and wearable numbers are the first-hand examples, and they sit in his own technical fields.

LN01 states the course purpose in full sentences: turn innovative robotics concepts into ventures; bridge engineering and market needs; produce a business model, a plan, a deck, and an elevator pitch. Assessment (LN01 slide 4): proposal 10%, development plan 20%, business plan 30%, investment deck 20%, final presentation 20%. The written plan is the heaviest artifact. The pitch, deck plus oral, is 40%.

## Model D — capital stack he makes students build

Source: LN10, especially slides 23 and 60–63. Book pp.90–94.

Hong Kong public-money slide (Chinese labels on an English deck): 創業資本企劃 government funding schemes, HKECIC trade credit, a startup budget sheet, youth entrepreneurship schemes, Y.E.S. self-employment support, business-plan advisory, "ask the expert," trade statistics, Labour Department hiring. The book adds ITF by name: grants are non-dilutive, must show milestones and impact, often take months, and should run in parallel with other cash so the company does not stall. A grant, he writes, can raise later valuation by de-risking the build. Angels, in the book, are often under HKD 5 million and bring a board voice. The HKD 5 million line is book-layer, not a slide.

The slide that is his teaching model is a robotic startup, not a SaaS template (LN10 slides 60–63):

- Twelve-month burn. Setup HKD 600,000. Year expense about HKD 2.91 million. Headcount of 4 is 34% of cost. R&D is 31%. Rent 8%. A buffer line is explicit.
- Income order: university funding (the row is labeled TSSSU in the margin) HKD 1,000,000 at setup and HKD 500,000 twice more (HKD 1,500,000). Government funding HKD 100,000 late in the year. Service income starts at month 7 (HKD 50,000 a month) and is marked "1st $ Point by service model." Component sales start later and are marked "1st $ Point by component sales." Product sales are not in year 1.
- Two HKD 1,000,000 investments, noted as a SAFE agreement (HKD 2,000,000 total).
- Years 2–5: product sales become the first-dollar-by-product line in year 2; breakeven is marked in year 2; dividends of HKD 8,000,000 and then 14,000,000; the ROI point is marked on the dividend line in year 3.
- Fundraising slide: angel SAFE with a 20% discount, valuation HKD 16,000,000, share 12.50, cheque HKD 2,000,000 in year 1. Series A valuation HKD 20,000,000, share 10, cheque HKD 2,000,000.

Hardware unit economics on slide 61: tooling HKD 300,000, test jigs, edge computer, PCB, SMT, ROS and an app, AWS. Orders of 100 / 200 / 500 / 1,000 robots. He prices at a 20% margin on total budget and shows gross profit. This is a classroom plan. It is not a term sheet he signed.

Book valuation tools (DCF, ownership = investment / post-money) are standard and sit under the same chapter. Y Combinator is the case (about USD 150,000 for 7%, batch mentoring). He uses YC as the picture of angel money plus incubation, which matches how he describes Hong Kong Science Park, Cyberport, and ITF in book pp.29–30.

## Model E — pitch theorems and his third framework

Source: LN12. Book pp.103–108.

LN12 slide 32 names three deck types: Tradition Framework, Storytelling Framework, and Alan's Scenario-based Framework. The scenario slides themselves are mostly images; the label is his. Slide 27 is titled "One Second Theorem." Slide 29 is "Zero Mistake Theorem" with the line "Prevent the Common Mistakes below" (the list did not extract). Slide 30 is "Demo in Technology Pitching."

The book defines the theorems in prose:

- One Second Theorem: each slide must land one idea in the first second. A 12-slide, 15-minute deck averages 75 seconds a slide, but first attention is shorter. Test: "What is the one idea this person should remember after just one look?"
- Zero Mistake Theorem: a wrong breakeven, a wrong TAM, a typo, or an inconsistent layout is a competence signal. His example: fixed cost HKD 500,000, price 100, variable 50, breakeven 10,000 units; showing 5,000 is a failed deck.
- Demo is king: 5 to 10 minutes, one core workflow, a quantified before/after, no dependency on a fragile network.
- Scenario pitching: angel hears team and what is unique; VC hears market size, growth, CAC. Elevator 30 seconds, deck 15 minutes, conversation 5 minutes. Same core problem, different numbers.

Cases he puts on the LN12 slides, not only Airbnb:

- NerOcean: sensors that cut sensor cost 80% and maintenance 90%, turning point measurement into 3D water-body monitoring; collaboration named with AFCD and SKLMP; a UN-endorsed 10-year estuarine project. Impact plus a cost claim plus a public scientific user.
- Meat the Next, founded 2020: plant-based "New Generation Food," carbon, sustainable lifestyle. He files it under Alan's scenario framework (slide 51).

Airbnb remains the book's exemplar for the theorems. Uber is the business-plan case. DJI, Dyson, Amazon, Tesla, Alibaba, Facebook, Xiaomi, YC are the chapter cases. The local cases on the pitch slides are a marine-sensor company and a food-impact company.

Book ch.11 adds: write the executive summary last; angels read passion and innovation, VCs read metrics and the exit, lenders read repayment; Virtual Room as a remote pitch space. Virtual Room is book-layer. The audience split matches LN12's three frameworks.
