ABSTRACT
This lecture book offers a comprehensive framework designed for postgraduate
students embarking on the exploration of startups and entrepreneurial ventures.
Comprising twelve detailed chapters, the content spans from foundational
principles of entrepreneurship to advanced topics including financial
management, marketing strategies, and investment pitching. The text integrates
real-world market information, rigorous mathematical analyses, and illustrative
step-by-step examples to enhance practical understanding.
The early chapters introduce entrepreneurship fundamentals, global economic
trends, and sustainability frameworks such as SDG and ESG, setting the stage
for innovation-driven business creation. Students gain insights into identifying
market opportunities using quantitative techniques such as TAM, SAM, and SOM
calculations, supported by competitive analysis models like SWOT and Porter's
Five Forces. The process of establishing a company's Vision, Mission, Values,
and lean startup methodology is explored with case studies exemplifying
successful strategies.
A key highlight is the emphasis on product commercialization through design
thinking, market targeting, and iterative validation, illustrated with Dyson's
transformational journey. The course then deeply investigates team formation
and management, product development lifecycle including minimum viable
products, and strategic setting of milestones and KPIs, providing students with
tools to manage operational complexities in fast-paced startup environments.
Subsequent chapters focus on business modeling and evaluation metrics
essential for startup viability assessment, supported by mathematical
formulations for break-even analysis and ROI calculations. Marketing and sales
strategies are dissected through frameworks like the 4P model and customer
segmentation, emphasizing practical approaches for customer acquisition and
retention.
Accounting and financial management fundamentals are covered extensively,
incorporating financial statement interpretation, budgeting, cash flow
management, and the utilization of modern accounting software such as Xero.
Budget planning and diverse funding channels including angel investors, venture
capital, crowdfunding, and grant applications are presented with detailed
valuation and financial forecasting techniques.
The final chapters address the composition of compelling business plans,
executive summaries, and innovative virtual collaboration tools. They culminate
in crafting effective investment decks and elevator pitches, balancing theoretical
underpinnings with pragmatic approaches supported by successful startup case
studies including Airbnb, Uber, and Facebook.

1.1. Introduction to Entrepreneurship
Entrepreneurship is the act of creating, developing, and managing a new
business venture to generate profit while assuming all associated risks. It plays a
critical role in economic growth, innovation, and job creation. Entrepreneurs
identify gaps in the marketplace and offer innovative solutions through new
products or services. Their ventures often introduce disruption, creating new
markets or refining existing ones.

At its core, entrepreneurship requires a blend of creativity, risk tolerance, and
strategic management. It thrives on the capacity to innovate and adapt to
changing market conditions. Within the startup ecosystem, entrepreneurs
spearhead ventures under conditions of uncertainty, leveraging customer
feedback, market knowledge, and lean methodologies to optimize their business
model.
The commercialisation process translates an entrepreneurial idea or invention
into a viable market offering. It spans from ideation through product development
to marketing and sales. Effective commercialisation demands understanding
customers’ needs, rigorous market research, and the ability to navigate
competitive forces. Technology startups especially face challenges in bridging
the gap between innovative products and scalable business models.

2. Identification of Market Opportunities

2.1. Market and Competitive Analysis
Understanding the market environment and competitive landscape is crucial for
startups seeking to identify and seize new opportunities effectively. Market
analysis involves assessing the market size, customer segments, and trends,
while competitive analysis evaluates the strengths, weaknesses, and strategies
of existing market players.
A detailed market and competitive analysis starts with defining the industry or
sector of interest. This definition includes identifying the geographical scope and
the product or service segment to be analyzed. By establishing clear boundaries,
startups can concentrate their efforts on relevant data and insights.
Evaluating market dynamics highlights opportunities and threats. Rising market
demand, emerging technologies, or regulatory changes can create openings for
innovative startups. Conversely, strong incumbent competitors or high entry
barriers can challenge new entrants.
Competitive analysis involves profiling existing players, their market share,
pricing, distribution channels, value propositions, and customer loyalty.
Understanding competitors' strengths allows startups to benchmark themselves
effectively. Examining weaknesses reveals gaps in market offerings that startups
can exploit.
Real market data and industry reports underpin accurate analysis. For example,
in the global electric vehicle (EV) market, Tesla's rise disrupted traditional
automotive giants by focusing on battery technology innovation and vertical
integration. Its ability to innovate quickly and control supply chains tilted
competitive advantage in its favor.
A comprehensive competitive analysis also includes indirect competitors and
substitute products, which may affect demand and market share. Additionally,
assessing market saturation and customer satisfaction levels further clarifies the
opportunity’s attractiveness.
Startups should use frameworks such as Porter's Five Forces alongside market
and competitor data to understand the competitive intensity and potential
profitability. This multi-dimensional analysis enables businesses to articulate a
viable market entry strategy that marries their capabilities with unmet market
needs. Incorporating real-world case studies deeply engrains these analytical
principles.

2.2. Market Research Techniques
Market research provides the raw data and insights foundational to identifying
meaningful opportunities. It combines qualitative and quantitative methods to
gather information about customers, competitors, and market conditions.
Primary research involves direct interactions with potential or existing customers
through surveys, interviews, focus groups, or observations. Gathering feedback
on needs, preferences, and buying behavior helps reveal gaps or emerging
trends. Startups can formulate questionnaires with closed and open-ended
questions to quantify customer opinions and motivations.
Secondary research analyzes existing data from reports, academic studies,
government databases, and industry associations. These sources offer valuable
macro-level insights, including market size, growth forecasts, demographic
trends, and competitor financials.
Combining both approaches yields a comprehensive picture. For example, a
startup planning to launch a technology product might use secondary data to
determine overall demand and primary research to understand customer pain
points and feature preferences.
Data collection precision and sampling represent critical success factors. Non-
representative samples or biased questions can distort findings. Ensuring
statistical validity and reliability supports accurate opportunity assessment.
Data analysis techniques include cross-tabulation, regression, and clustering,
which identify relationships and segment customers. Visualization tools like
charts and heatmaps make complex data easier to interpret.

2.3. Market Size Calculation (TAM/SAM/SOM)
Market sizing quantifies the revenue opportunity a startup can target. The TAM-
SAM-SOM model is widely recognized for structuring this estimation.
•    Total Addressable Market (TAM) is the overall revenue opportunity if a
     product or service reached all potential customers globally, without
     constraints.
•    Serviceable Available Market (SAM) narrows TAM to the segment that
     matches the startup’s product capabilities and geographic reach.
•    Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) further refines SAM to the portion
     the startup can realistically capture, considering competitors and operational
     capacity.
Calculating TAM begins by defining the target market scope clearly. For example,
for a new electric vehicle, the market might be defined by geography, vehicle
type, and customer demographics.

One method to calculate TAM is the top-down approach, where industry reports
provide the total market revenue, which is then segmented.
The bottom-up approach estimates TAM by multiplying the number of potential
customers by the average selling price (ASP).
For example, consider calculating TAM for an EV startup targeting Hong Kong’s
market. Suppose there are approximately 600,000 registered private vehicles,
and the ASP for a new EV is HKD 300,000.
𝑇𝐴𝑀 = Number of vehicles × ASP = 600,000 × 300,000 = 180,000,000,000 HKD
This reflects the maximum obtainable market size in Hong Kong alone.
SAM then restricts this to feasible customers, such as those eligible to purchase
a new EV considering income and charging infrastructure access. Suppose SAM
comprises 30% of TAM customers:
        𝑆𝐴𝑀 = 𝑇𝐴𝑀 × 0.3 = 180,000,000,000 × 0.3 = 54,000,000,000 HKD
SOM reflects the share realistically captured by factoring competition and
capacity. Assuming the startup can capture 5% of SAM in the first years:
        𝑆𝑂𝑀 = 𝑆𝐴𝑀 × 0.05 = 54,000,000,000 × 0.05 = 2,700,000,000 HKD
The stepwise refinement aids strategic planning and investment decisions.
Market sizing is a moving target influenced by technology, consumer preferences,
and regulation. Continuous updating with new data maintains accuracy.

2.4. Market Analysis Tools: SWOT, PEST, Porter's Five
     Forces
Several analytical frameworks assist in deepening market understanding.
SWOT Analysis identifies Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats.
It's simple yet powerful for evaluating internal capabilities and external market
environments. For instance, Tesla’s strengths include innovative battery
technology; weaknesses might be high production costs. Opportunities
encompass growing EV demand; threats include regulatory impacts and
competitors.
PEST Analysis examines Political, Economic, Social, and Technological factors
impacting markets. For example:
•    Political: Government incentives for EV adoption.
•    Economic: Fluctuations in commodity prices affecting battery costs.
•    Social: Increasing environmental awareness.
•    Technological: Breakthroughs in battery density.
This tool helps anticipate macro-environmental trends influencing industry
attractiveness.
Porter's Five Forces explore competitive intensity via five dimensions:
1.   Competitive Rivalry: The number and strength of competitors. Tesla faces
     strong traditional automakers transitioning to EVs.
2.   Threat of New Entrants: Barriers like capital requirements reduce new
     players.
3.   Bargaining Power of Suppliers: Tesla’s vertical integration reduces
     dependence on external suppliers.
4.   Bargaining Power of Buyers: Buyer concentration or price sensitivity
     affects pricing power.
5.   Threat of Substitutes: Alternative transport modes may threaten market
     share.
Applying these tools provides structured insight into operating conditions,
supporting strategic navigation.

6.1. Whole Product Canvas Methodology
The Whole Product Canvas is a comprehensive framework designed to help
startups, entrepreneurs, and product teams visualize and organize all critical
aspects of a product to ensure successful development and market fit. It covers
the product’s core features, user needs, business objectives, and market
dynamics. The methodology guides founders through a structured exploration of
their product’s value proposition, customer segments, channels, key activities,
and the ecosystem within which the product operates.
At the core of the Whole Product Canvas is the understanding that delivering a
successful product involves more than just building features. It requires the
alignment of customer needs with product offerings, supported by a viable
business model and operational capabilities. By visualizing this alignment on a
single canvas, teams can detect gaps, prioritize development efforts, and align
stakeholders early in the product lifecycle.
Key components of the canvas include:
•    Customer Segments: Identification of primary and secondary customer
     groups. Understanding jobs-to-be-done, pain points, and desired gains.
•    Value Propositions: Clear articulation of how the product solves customer
     problems or fulfills opportunities.
•    Channels: Selection of distribution and communication pathways to reach
     customers effectively.
•    Customer Relationships: Defining how the business interacts, services,
     and retains customers.
•    Key Activities: Critical operations required to deliver value.
•    Key Resources: Assets and capabilities essential for product development
     and delivery.
•    Key Partnerships: External parties that enable or enhance the product
     offering.
•    Revenue Streams and Cost Structure: Financial aspects crucial for
     sustainability.
A practical application involves mapping features into “must-have,” “nice-to-have,”
and “future enhancements,” ensuring a focus on the Minimum Viable Product
(MVP) that resonates with the customers' most pressing needs. For example, a
startup designing a coworking space booking system used the canvas to identify
their core value proposition was “simple, reliable room booking,” steering their
product design strictly to those features [2].

6.2. Minimum Viable Product Development
A Minimum Viable Product (MVP) is the most stripped-down version of a product
that can still effectively deliver value to early customers and enable learning for
iterative development. The core principle of MVP development is to test
hypotheses around product-market fit with the least resource investment possible,
accelerating feedback loops and reducing the risk of expensive failure.
Eric Ries popularized the MVP concept in The Lean Startup, describing it as the
quickest, cheapest way to produce an initial product version to validate
assumptions about what customers want [5]. In product engineering, this involves
balancing the need for functionality with simplicity and speed.
Key steps in MVP development include:
•    Define Learning Goals: Identify what you want to learn from early users.
     For example, does a coworking booking app meet real user needs in ease of
     reservation?
•    Prioritize Core Features: Focus on functionalities that address critical
     problems or reveal core user behaviors.
•    Build the MVP Quickly: Employ rapid prototyping methodologies, using
     minimal design and technology complexity.
•    Test with Real Users: Deploy the MVP to a controlled target audience and
     collect qualitative and quantitative feedback.
•    Iterate or Pivot: Use insights to enhance the product or alter strategic
     direction.

11.1.Introduction to Business Plan
A business plan is a formal written document outlining a company's goals,
strategies, market analysis, financial projections, and operational approach. It
serves as a roadmap for the startup’s growth and development. For
entrepreneurs, a strong business plan clarifies objectives, guides decision-
making, and attracts investment and partners.
Business plans provide a structured approach. They lay out the venture’s value
proposition and market opportunity clearly. The core components include the
executive summary, market analysis, product or service description, operational
plan, financial plan, and risk assessment. Each section demands thorough
research and precise presentation.
Startups face uncertainty. A business plan reduces this by defining strategies in
advance. It identifies target customers and competitors, foresees financial needs,
and outlines marketing approaches. A well-articulated plan shows
professionalism, thus gaining trust from stakeholders.
Using real data is crucial. For example, market size statistics, revenue forecasts,
and cost estimates must rely on current information to maintain credibility. This
data helps calculate feasibility and potential returns. Without substantiated data,
investment interest wanes.
A business plan also facilitates internal communication. Team members align
their activities toward common goals, fostering efficiency and coherence. It
supports monitoring progress via milestones, ensuring adaptability when
challenges arise.
Mathematically, a business plan integrates financial calculations such as break-
even analysis, cash flow projections, and return on investment (ROI). These
quantify performance and risk. For instance, break-even analysis determines the
sales volume needed to cover fixed and variable costs, indicating when
profitability begins.
Consider an example: Suppose a startup has fixed costs of HKD 200,000 yearly,
variable cost per unit of HKD 50, and sells products at HKD 100 each. The
break-even point in units is computed as:
                                         Fixed Costs                    200,000
  Break-even units =                                                  =
                       Selling Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit 100 − 50
= 4,000 units
This calculation informs how many units must be sold to avoid losses. It guides
pricing and sales strategies.

language and compelling statements.

stability and repayment capacity.

12.2.One Second Theorem: Slide Impact
The "One Second Theorem" emphasizes the critical impact of each pitch deck
slide within the first second. Investors and stakeholders often decide almost
instantly whether to engage with the presentation content or lose interest. A slide
must communicate its core message within that brief window.
Developing slides with concise, powerful headlines and digestible visuals is
essential. Overloading slides with text or complex graphs delays comprehension,
reducing impact. Clear, bold statements paired with supporting images or simple
data charts optimize immediate understanding.
Airbnb’s pitch deck illustrated this concept. Their initial slide addressed three
primary traveler pain points instantly: high cost, lack of local experiences, and
limited accommodations. The problem statement was visually and verbally sharp,
enticing investors to continue. This approach aligns perfectly with the One
Second Theorem [1].
Designing slide content respecting this theorem involves editing out non-
essential information rigorously. Each slide should answer: “What is the one idea
this person should remember after just one look?”
Mathematically, if a pitch deck contains 12 slides and an investor spends 15
minutes, the average time per slide is:
                       15 × 60 seconds
                                       = 75 seconds per slide
                              12
However, initial attention per slide can be far less due to cognitive overload and
competing stimuli. Hence condensing messages to fit initial one-second impact
increases engagement.
Use contrast, bold typography, and icons or visuals to enhance message
retention. Color choices can guide focus instantly. Prioritize clarity over density.

12.3.Zero Mistake Theorem for Pitch Decks
The Zero Mistake Theorem posits that each slide in a pitch deck must be free
from errors or ambiguity—no factual inaccuracies, spelling mistakes, or design
inconsistencies. Small errors undermine credibility and create doubt in investor
minds.
Startups must rigorously proofread all content, verify data accuracy, and apply
consistent formatting. Mistakes can range from wrong financial figures to unclear
slide titles. Financial projections are often scrutinized closely. One error in a key
metric like break-even point could cause investors to question management
competency.
Ensuring data precision involves cross-checking calculations, sourcing figures
from validated industry reports or internal analytics, and revising iteratively. For
example, if presenting total addressable market (TAM) figures, cite reliable
market research to justify the numbers.

12.4.Demo Presentation Techniques
An interactive demo is often the centerpiece of a pitch, illustrating product
functionality and user experience in real time. “Demo is King” underscores the
value of a reliable demo in converting investor interest into funding commitments.
Effective demos showcase key features that solve market problems distinctly.
They must run smoothly to avoid interruptions or technical glitches, which distract
and erode pitch credibility.
Preparation starts with defining clear demo objectives aligned with pitch goals.
Focus on demonstrating the product’s unique value proposition within a short
time frame, typically 5 to 10 minutes.
Practicing the demo stage extensively reduces risks. Anticipate questions and
incorporate scripted responses for anticipated challenges or failures.
A practical example involves a tech startup presenting a SaaS platform. Instead
of navigating every feature, the demo highlights a core workflow that saves
customers time or money. Quantify benefits during the demo: "Our software
reduces client onboarding time from 10 days to 2 days, lowering costs by 80%."
Ensure the demo environment mimics real-world usage conditions. Avoid
reliance on internet speed or external dependencies that might fail.
Airbnb’s success partly derived from live walkthroughs showing how easy hosts
and guests can connect and book accommodations. Investors saw firsthand the
platform's smooth process and potential for viral growth [3].
Effective demo presentation utilizes clear narration, guided pointers, and
engaging visuals. Balancing detail with simplicity ensures the audience grasps
the innovation and potential quickly.
Demos must also segue smoothly into Q&A or discussion, where deeper investor
concerns can be addressed.
